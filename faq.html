Frequently asked questions

Your kidney questions, answered.

General information to help you understand kidney health. For advice about your own condition, please book a consultation.

What does a nephrologist treat?

A nephrologist is a doctor who specialises in the kidneys. This includes chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, kidney-related high blood pressure, diabetic kidney disease, protein or blood in the urine, electrolyte problems, dialysis and kidney transplant care.

When should I see a nephrologist instead of my family doctor?

If your creatinine is raised, your urine shows protein or blood, you have swelling, your blood pressure is hard to control, or you have had diabetes for many years, a nephrologist can find the cause and plan treatment. Your family doctor may also refer you.

Does Dr. Gupta treat children?

Dr. Sachin Gupta treats adult nephrology patients. For children with kidney problems, please consult a paediatric nephrologist.

Can chronic kidney disease be reversed?

Lost kidney function usually cannot be fully regained, but the progression of CKD can often be slowed significantly. Good control of blood pressure and sugar, the right medicines, diet changes and avoiding kidney-harming drugs all make a real difference — especially when started early.

What do my creatinine and eGFR numbers mean?

Creatinine is a waste product the kidneys clear from the blood; eGFR is an estimate of how well the kidneys are filtering, calculated from creatinine, age and sex. A lower eGFR means lower kidney function. Dr. Gupta will explain your numbers and your CKD stage at the consultation.

Are painkillers safe for my kidneys?

Common painkillers such as NSAIDs (for example ibuprofen and diclofenac) can harm the kidneys, especially in people with CKD, diabetes or dehydration. Always check with your doctor before taking regular painkillers or herbal remedies.

Does starting dialysis mean my kidneys will never work again?

Not always. In acute kidney injury, dialysis may be needed only for a short time while the kidneys recover. In advanced chronic kidney disease, dialysis is usually long-term unless a kidney transplant is done.

What is the difference between haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis?

Haemodialysis cleans the blood using a machine, usually three sessions a week at a dialysis unit. Peritoneal dialysis uses the lining of your abdomen as a filter and can be done at home every day after training. The right choice depends on your health, lifestyle and preferences.

What is a perm cath?

A perm cath is a tunnelled dialysis catheter placed into a large vein in the neck and run under the skin. It gives reliable access for haemodialysis while a fistula matures or when longer-term access is needed quickly. Keep the site clean and dry, and report fever or redness promptly.

Who can get a kidney transplant?

Many people with kidney failure can be considered for a transplant after a careful evaluation of their heart, infections, other illnesses and overall fitness. A suitable living donor (often a family member) or a deceased-donor kidney is needed.

What care is needed after a kidney transplant?

After a transplant you will take anti-rejection (immunosuppressive) medicines every day and need regular blood tests and reviews for life. Long-term follow-up helps protect the new kidney and catch problems early.

Bring your recent blood and urine reports, any ultrasound or scan reports, discharge summaries from past hospital stays, and a list (or the strips) of every medicine you currently take.
